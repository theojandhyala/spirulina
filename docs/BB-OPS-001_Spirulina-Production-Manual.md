# What is spirulina?

A cyanobacterium — not a plant and not an algae — and that distinction shapes
everything else in this manual. What the organism actually is, how it is built,
why its filament length is worth money, what it contains, and two nutrition
claims the trade routinely gets wrong.

## 1. What spirulina actually is

### 1.1 It is a bacterium, not a plant and not an algae

Spirulina is a **cyanobacterium** — a photosynthetic bacterium. It is a
prokaryote: it has no nucleus, no chloroplasts, no mitochondria, and none of
the membrane-bound organelles that define plant and algal cells.

This is not a technicality. It has three practical consequences that shape
everything else in this manual:

| Consequence | Why it matters in production |
| --- | --- |
| **No cellulose cell wall** | The wall is thin peptidoglycan. Spirulina is digestible without any cell disruption step. *Chlorella*, a true green alga, needs its wall cracked mechanically before humans can absorb it. That processing step is a large capital and energy cost we do not have. |
| **Prokaryotic simplicity** | Growth is fast and nutrient demand is straightforward. It responds to changes in hours, not days — good when you are correcting a problem, dangerous when you are causing one. |
| **Bacterial physiology** | It is vulnerable to the same things that suppress bacteria — pH, salinity, ammonia — which is precisely what gives us contamination control without chemicals. |

### 1.2 Naming: *Spirulina* versus *Arthrospira*

| Name | Status |
| --- | --- |
| ***Arthrospira platensis*** | The correct scientific name of the commercial organism |
| ***Arthrospira maxima*** | The other commercial species, common in Central American strains |
| *Spirulina* | A **separate genus**, not what is sold as spirulina |
| "Spirulina" | The trade and common name, universally used, legally accepted |

The organism was originally placed in *Spirulina*, then moved to *Arthrospira*
on the basis of trichome structure. The food trade kept the old name. So the
product is called spirulina and the organism is *Arthrospira*. Both are correct
in their own context. On a certificate of analysis, write *Arthrospira
platensis*; on a retail label, write spirulina.

### 1.3 Shape and structure

Spirulina grows as a **trichome** — a multicellular filament — coiled into an
open left-handed helix. It is the helix that gives the organism its name and,
as it happens, its commercial viability.

| Feature | Typical value |
| --- | --- |
| Trichome width | 6 – 12 µm |
| Trichome length | 100 – 500 µm |
| Helix diameter | 30 – 70 µm |
| Helix pitch | 40 – 80 µm |
| Number of cells per trichome | 20 – 100+ |

**⚠ The filament length is the single most commercially important physical
property of this organism.** At 100–500 µm, a spirulina filament can be caught
on a fine screen. A single-celled alga at 5–10 µm cannot — it needs a centrifuge.
Screen filtration costs a fraction of centrifugation in both capital and running
energy. Everything in Part 5 of this manual depends on the filaments staying
long and intact, which is why shear damage is treated so seriously.

### 1.4 Gas vesicles and buoyancy

Spirulina cells contain **gas vesicles** — protein-walled, gas-filled structures
that reduce cell density and provide buoyancy. In a still pond the culture
floats towards the surface.

| Effect | Consequence |
| --- | --- |
| Buoyancy | Culture rises when circulation stops; forms a surface scum |
| Light exposure | Buoyant cells sit in the highest light, where they photoinhibit |
| Harvest | Assists flotation-based harvesting where used |
| Collapse under pressure | Vigorous pumping can collapse vesicles, changing buoyancy |

This is one reason circulation runs continuously. A pond left unstirred for a
few hours in daylight concentrates its own biomass into the most damaging light
at the surface.

### 1.5 The helix is not fixed

Under stress — temperature extremes, nutrient limitation, some contamination —
trichomes can **uncoil into a straight or loosely waved form**. This is the
*linear morphotype*.

| Point | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| It is usually reversible | Restore conditions and coiling usually returns over several generations |
| It is a diagnostic signal | Uncoiling under the microscope means something is wrong before density falls |
| It can become fixed | Some strains lose coiling permanently after prolonged stress |
| It affects harvest | Straight filaments screen slightly differently; heavy fragmentation screens poorly |

**Watch the shape, not only the count.** Morphology changes days before yield
does.

### 1.6 How it reproduces

Spirulina does not have sex, spores, or a complex life cycle. It multiplies by:

1. **Binary fission** — cells divide across the trichome, lengthening it.
2. **Fragmentation** — specialised cells called **necridia** die and lyse at
   intervals along the trichome, breaking it into short motile fragments called
   **hormogonia**.
3. Each hormogonium grows into a full trichome.

Practical meaning: a healthy culture contains a **spread of filament lengths**.
A culture of uniformly very short fragments is either young from a recent
transfer, or is fragmenting from stress or mechanical damage.

### 1.7 What it does and does not do metabolically

| Capability | Status | Why you care |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Oxygenic photosynthesis | Yes | It makes oxygen; supersaturation in a stagnant pond is a real inhibition risk |
| Carbon from bicarbonate | Yes, via a carbon-concentrating mechanism | This is why a bicarbonate-buffered alkaline medium works so well |
| **Nitrogen fixation** | **No** | ⚠ A widespread myth. *Arthrospira* has no heterocysts and cannot fix atmospheric nitrogen. **All nitrogen must be supplied.** A culture starved of nitrogen will bleach and stall — it cannot rescue itself from the air |
| Nitrate uptake | Yes | The standard nitrogen source |
| Ammonium uptake | Yes, and preferentially | Cheap, but toxic above threshold at high pH — see § 3.6 |
| Urea uptake | Yes, after hydrolysis to ammonium | Cheapest per kg N, needs careful dosing |
| Heterotrophic growth in the dark | Essentially no | It will not grow on sugar in a fermenter |

### 1.8 Where it grows naturally

Spirulina is native to **alkaline soda lakes** — shallow, warm, highly alkaline
waters with dissolved carbonate and bicarbonate, high sodium, and pH between
about 8.5 and 11.

| Lake | Region | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lake Texcoco | Valley of Mexico | Harvested by the Mexica (Aztecs) as **tecuitlatl** |
| Lake Chad | Central Africa | Still harvested and dried into cakes called **dihé** by the Kanembu |
| Rift Valley lakes | Kenya, Ethiopia, Tanzania | Natural blooms; the food source of lesser flamingos |

**This natural habitat is the whole basis of open-pond spirulina farming.** Very
few organisms tolerate pH 10 and 15 g/L of dissolved salts. By reproducing a
soda lake in a lined pond, we get a growth medium that is close to a selective
one — the chemistry that suits the crop actively suppresses most competitors.
No other food crop offers anything comparable.

### 1.9 A long history as food

| Period | Record |
| --- | --- |
| Pre-Columbian | The Mexica harvested tecuitlatl from Lake Texcoco, dried it into cakes and sold it in the market at Tenochtitlan; recorded by chroniclers of the Spanish conquest |
| Traditional and current | The Kanembu people around Lake Chad harvest, sun-dry and eat dihé, typically in a sauce with millet |
| 1960s–70s | Rediscovered by European researchers studying Lake Chad; commercial cultivation begins |
| 1970s–present | Industrial cultivation in Mexico, USA (California, Hawaii), China, India, Thailand, Chile |

Spirulina is not a modern invention. It is one of the oldest documented
harvested foods still in commercial production.

## 2. Composition and nutrition — stated honestly

### 2.1 Gross composition

Typical ranges for well-grown, properly washed and dried commercial spirulina,
on a dry-weight basis:

| Component | Typical range | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Protein | 55 – 70 % | Among the highest of any whole food; varies with nitrogen availability |
| Carbohydrate | 15 – 25 % | Mostly glycogen and rhamnose polymers; low simple sugar |
| Lipid | 5 – 8 % | Includes gamma-linolenic acid (GLA), typically ~1 % of dry weight |
| Ash (minerals) | 6 – 9 % | ⚠ Strongly dependent on washing — poorly washed biomass carries medium salts and fails specification |
| Moisture | ≤ 7 % | Specification limit; product is hygroscopic |
| Nucleic acids | 4 – 6 % | See § 2.5 — this is what limits sensible daily intake |
| Fibre | 3 – 6 % | Low; not a fibre source |

### 2.2 Protein quality

Spirulina contains **all nine essential amino acids**, which makes it a complete
protein. It is not, however, perfectly balanced.

| Amino acid | Status in spirulina |
| --- | --- |
| Methionine and cysteine | **Limiting** — the sulphur amino acids are the weak point |
| Lysine | Moderate |
| Leucine, isoleucine, valine | Good |
| Tryptophan, threonine, phenylalanine | Adequate |

Protein is conventionally calculated as **nitrogen × 6.25**. Note that this
overstates true protein in spirulina, because a meaningful fraction of the
nitrogen sits in nucleic acids and phycocyanin rather than in dietary protein.
Some references use a factor of 5.95 or lower. **State the factor used on the
certificate of analysis** — otherwise your figure is not comparable to anyone
else's.

### 2.3 The pigments

Spirulina's colour comes from three pigment groups, and they are commercially
significant in their own right.

| Pigment | Colour | Typical % dry wt | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Phycocyanin** | Brilliant blue | 10 – 20 % | The signature pigment. Water-soluble. Sold as a natural blue food colour at a large multiple of biomass price. **Heat and light labile** |
| Chlorophyll *a* | Green | ~1 % | Fat-soluble |
| Carotenoids | Orange-yellow | 0.3 – 0.5 % | Beta-carotene, zeaxanthin, others |

The blue-green colour is phycocyanin plus chlorophyll together. **Colour is your
cheapest quality indicator**: strong blue-green means intact phycocyanin, which
means the culture was healthy and the drying was controlled. Dull, olive, grey or
brownish powder means pigment has been lost — usually to heat in drying, light in
storage, or a stressed culture.

### 2.4 Vitamins and minerals — with one important correction

| Nutrient | Present | Comment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Iron | Yes, significant | Relatively bioavailable |
| Calcium, magnesium, potassium | Yes | |
| Zinc, selenium, manganese | Trace | Depends on medium and water |
| Beta-carotene (provitamin A) | Yes, significant | |
| Vitamin K | Yes | |
| Vitamin E | Yes | |
| Thiamine, riboflavin, niacin, B6 | Yes | |
| **Vitamin B12** | ⚠ **NO — see below** | |

**⚠ THE B12 POINT — get this right, it matters legally and ethically.**

Spirulina analyses as rich in vitamin B12 by older microbiological assays. It is
**not** a usable B12 source for humans. What it contains is largely
**pseudocobalamin** — a B12 analogue that human physiology cannot use, and
which may even interfere with absorption of genuine B12.

**Never market spirulina as a source of vitamin B12, and never tell a customer
— especially a vegan or vegetarian customer — that it will meet their B12
needs.** Doing so is false, and it could cause real harm to somebody relying on
it. This is the single most common false claim in the spirulina trade.

### 2.5 Why you should not eat unlimited quantities

Spirulina is 4–6 % nucleic acid. Nucleic acids metabolise to **purines**, and
purines to **uric acid**. High sustained intake of any single-cell protein raises
uric acid load, which matters for gout and for kidney stone formation.

The conventional guidance for single-cell protein foods is to keep intake to
roughly **20 g per day or less** for an adult. Typical spirulina serving sizes of
3–10 g/day sit comfortably inside that.

This is a reason to state serving sizes on the label and not to encourage
unlimited consumption.

### 2.6 What we do not claim

Spirulina is a nutritious food. It is not a medicine.

**⚠ Do not make, print, imply, or repeat any claim that spirulina treats, cures,
prevents or mitigates any disease.** Doing so is unlawful under Indian food law
and in every export market that matters. It is also unnecessary: the honest
composition above is a strong enough proposition on its own.

Specific cautions worth knowing and being able to answer:

| Group | Caution |
| --- | --- |
| Phenylketonuria (PKU) | Spirulina is high in phenylalanine and is unsuitable |
| Autoimmune conditions | Immune-stimulating effects are reported; advise medical consultation |
| Immunosuppressant medication | Same reason; advise medical consultation |
| Pregnancy | Insufficient evidence for a positive recommendation; advise medical consultation |
| Gout / hyperuricaemia | See § 2.5 |
| Anyone relying on B12 | See § 2.4 |

# What does it need to grow?

Six variables: light, temperature, carbon, pH and alkalinity, nutrients and
mixing. Keep all six inside their window and the crop largely looks after
itself. Why a rising pH is the sign of a healthy culture, why the alkaline
medium is really a contamination barrier, and what productivity you can
honestly expect.

## 3. The six things spirulina needs

Everything in cultivation reduces to keeping six variables inside a window.
Learn these and you understand the whole crop.

| # | Requirement | Target | Failure looks like |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **Light** | 150 – 200 µmol/m²/s at the surface | Too little: pale, slow, thin. Too much: bleached, photoinhibited |
| 2 | **Temperature** | 35 – 37 °C | Below 20: stalls. Above 42: dies |
| 3 | **Carbon** | Bicarbonate 8–16 g/L, CO₂ replenished | pH climbs, growth stops |
| 4 | **pH and alkalinity** | pH 9.5 – 10.5 | Below 8.5: contamination. Above 11: inhibition |
| 5 | **Nutrients** | N, P, K, S, Mg, Fe + traces | Yellowing, stalling, thin culture |
| 6 | **Mixing** | 0.25 m/s, continuous | Settling, stratification, surface scum, oxygen build-up |

### 3.1 Light

| Parameter | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Saturation intensity | 150 – 200 µmol photons/m²/s PAR |
| Photoinhibition begins | Above roughly 500 µmol/m²/s on thin or dilute culture |
| Full tropical midday sun | 1,800 – 2,200 µmol/m²/s |
| Compensation point | Low; the culture respires at night and loses a little biomass |

Note what those numbers mean together: **midday sun is roughly ten times the
saturation intensity.** Spirulina is not short of light in Telangana. It is at
risk of too much.

Protection comes from the culture protecting itself:

- **Density** — a dense culture self-shades. Cells at the surface shade those
  below, and mixing rotates them through.
- **Depth** — light falls off exponentially. At 200 mm and working density, the
  bottom of the pond is nearly dark.
- **Mixing** — every cell spends a short time in high light and longer in shade.
  This flashing-light effect is more efficient than constant moderate light.

<figure class="diagram" data-diagram="lightdepth">
<figcaption>Live: cross-section through 200 mm of culture. Light falls away exponentially with depth; cells circulate between the bright surface and the dark floor. Each cell brightens as it rises — the flashing-light effect.</figcaption>
</figure>

**⚠ A dilute culture in full sun is in danger.** After a harvest that has taken
density too low, or after rain dilution, the culture has lost its own shading
and will bleach. That is the moment to consider increasing depth or accepting a
slower harvest.

### 3.2 Temperature

| Range | Behaviour |
| --- | --- |
| Below 15 °C | Effectively dormant; extended cold causes losses |
| 15 – 20 °C | Survives, minimal growth |
| 20 – 25 °C | Slow growth |
| 25 – 35 °C | Good growth |
| **35 – 37 °C** | **Optimum** |
| 38 – 40 °C | Growth continues but stress begins |
| 40 – 42 °C | Damage; sustained exposure is dangerous |
| Above 42 – 45 °C | **Death** |

**⚠ This is the principal climate risk at this site.** The design ambient
maximum is 45 °C. A shallow, dark, well-lit pond can sit several degrees above
ambient air temperature. Mitigations, in order of preference:

1. **Increase depth** — more water, more thermal mass, slower heating.
2. **Maintain density** — a dense culture absorbs light near the surface.
3. **Harvest early** — take the harvest in the morning, not the afternoon.
4. **Shading** — evaluated at design stage.
5. **Evaporative loss** helps a little; make-up water is cooler.

Night cooling is normally sufficient in a dry climate. The dangerous condition is
a run of consecutive very hot days with warm nights.

### 3.3 Carbon and the pH relationship

This is the least intuitive part of spirulina cultivation and the one most worth
understanding properly.

Spirulina takes up carbon from **bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻)** in the medium. As it
consumes carbon, the carbonate–bicarbonate equilibrium shifts and **the pH
rises**:

```
CO₂ + H₂O  ⇌  H₂CO₃  ⇌  HCO₃⁻ + H⁺  ⇌  CO₃²⁻ + 2H⁺
```

Remove carbon from the middle of that chain and the system moves towards
carbonate — and alkaline.

**Therefore: rising pH is the signal that the culture is growing and consuming
carbon.**

| Observation | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| pH climbing steadily through the day | Healthy, actively photosynthesising culture |
| pH flat in full daylight | ⚠ Culture is not growing — investigate immediately |
| pH climbing past 11 | Carbon is exhausted; growth will stop |
| pH falling | CO₂ over-dosing, or the culture is dying and respiration dominates |

You correct rising pH by **injecting CO₂**, which pushes the equilibrium back
towards bicarbonate and regenerates the carbon supply. This is why CO₂ dosing is
controlled on the pH signal — pH is a direct, real-time proxy for carbon demand.

**⚠ Inject CO₂ only in daylight.** There is no photosynthesis at night, so
night-time CO₂ is wasted, and it drives pH down towards the range where
contaminants thrive.

<figcaption>Live: one day in a pond. Light drives photosynthesis, carbon is consumed, pH climbs. CO₂ dosing pulses push it back down. At night photosynthesis stops and pH goes flat — which is why dosing stops too.</figcaption>
</figure>

### 3.4 Alkalinity and salinity — the contamination barrier

| Parameter | Target | Range |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Total alkalinity as NaHCO₃ | 12 g/L | 8 – 16 g/L |
| pH | 9.8 | 9.5 – 10.5 |
| Salinity (TDS) | 18 g/L | 12 – 30 g/L |

**Treat these three as a contamination-control system, not merely as growth
conditions.**

Almost nothing else that would want to live in a warm, nutrient-rich, brightly
lit pond can tolerate pH 10 with 12 g/L bicarbonate and 18 g/L dissolved solids.
That chemistry is doing the job that a pesticide would do in a conventional crop,
for the cost of sodium bicarbonate.

**⚠ Every proposal to cut bicarbonate cost by running lower alkalinity is a
proposal to weaken your only contamination barrier.** It will look like a saving
for several weeks and then cost you a pond. If you must reduce it, do so on one
pond, watch it under the microscope daily, and be ready to restore.

Salinity also rises on its own, because evaporation leaves salts behind while the
water leaves. That is why blowdown exists — see § 12.4.

### 3.5 Nutrients

Spirulina biomass has a reasonably consistent elemental composition, so nutrient
demand can be calculated directly from expected production.

| Element | % of dry biomass | Supplied as |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Carbon | 46 % | CO₂ / bicarbonate |
| Nitrogen | 10.5 % | Sodium nitrate or urea |
| Phosphorus | 1.1 % | Di-potassium phosphate |
| Potassium | 1.5 % | Phosphate and sulphate salts |
| Sulphur | 0.8 % | Potassium and magnesium sulphates |
| Magnesium | 0.3 % | Magnesium sulphate |
| Iron | 0.1 % | Ferrous sulphate, chelated |
| Calcium | 0.1 – 0.4 % | Calcium chloride, or from the water |
| Micronutrients | Trace | B, Mn, Zn, Cu, Mo |

**To feed a pond, work from what you harvested.** Every kilogram of dry biomass
removed took roughly 105 g of nitrogen and 11 g of phosphorus out with it. Put
that back. Worked example in § 25.3.

**⚠ Iron deficiency is the most commonly missed one.** Iron precipitates readily
at pH 10, which is why it is dosed as a chelate with EDTA. A culture that is
pale, slow and unresponsive despite adequate nitrogen is very often short of
available iron.

### 3.6 Ammonia — useful and dangerous

If urea or an ammonium salt is used as the nitrogen source, it enters the culture
as ammonium (NH₄⁺). At high pH, ammonium converts to **free ammonia (NH₃)**,
which is toxic to spirulina.

The equilibrium is strongly pH-dependent:

| pH | Approximate fraction as free NH₃ at 30 °C |
| --- | --- |
| 8.0 | ~ 5 % |
| 9.0 | ~ 35 % |
| 10.0 | ~ 80 % |
| 10.5 | ~ 92 % |

**⚠ At our operating pH, almost all ammonium is present as free ammonia.** A dose
of urea that would be harmless at pH 8 can be lethal at pH 10.

Rules if urea is used:

1. Dose **little and often** — daily or more frequently, never a weekly slug.
2. Dose into **moving** water, downstream of the paddle wheel, never a static pond.
3. Keep free ammonia below about **2 mg/L**.
4. Add free ammonia to the routine monitoring schedule.
5. Never dose urea to a culture already stressed or at unusually high pH.

Sodium nitrate avoids this problem entirely, at higher cost. That trade-off is
the reason § 3.5 of the design criteria leaves the decision to trial results
rather than to price.

### 3.7 Mixing

| Parameter | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Design channel velocity | 0.25 m/s |
| Acceptable range | 0.20 – 0.30 m/s |
| Minimum to prevent settling | 0.15 m/s |
| Operating regime | Continuous, 24 h |

Mixing does five jobs at once:

1. Rotates cells between light at the surface and shade below.
2. Keeps biomass suspended — spirulina settles and, worse, floats.
3. Prevents thermal stratification.
4. Strips dissolved oxygen, which inhibits photosynthesis when supersaturated.
5. Distributes nutrients and CO₂.

**⚠ Never stop circulation in daylight.** Within an hour the culture floats into
maximum light and photoinhibits; within a few hours it is settling, stratifying
and accumulating oxygen. Loss of paddle wheels is the fastest way to lose a pond,
which is why they sit on backup power.

Faster is not better. Above about 0.35 m/s the extra energy buys nothing and the
shear begins to fragment filaments — which then screen poorly at harvest.

## 4. Growth rate and what to expect

### 4.1 The growth curve

| Phase | What happens | Typical duration after inoculation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Lag** | Culture adapts; little visible change | 1 – 3 days |
| **Exponential** | Rapid doubling; pH climbs strongly | 3 – 10 days |
| **Linear** | Light-limited by self-shading; steady daily increment | The normal operating state |
| **Stationary** | Growth balances losses | Reached if harvesting stops |
| **Decline** | Nutrient exhaustion, pH extremes or contamination | Failure state |

**A production pond is held in the linear phase.** You harvest daily or every few
days to keep density in the band where growth per unit area is highest, and you
never let it reach stationary.

### 4.2 Realistic productivity

| Measure | Realistic outdoor commercial | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Areal productivity, good day | 10 – 15 g/m²/day | |
| Areal productivity, annual mean | 6 – 10 g/m²/day | Includes bad weather, cleaning, restarts |
| Volumetric | 0.05 – 0.08 g/L/day | At 200 mm depth |
| Doubling time, laboratory optimum | ~ 24 h | Not achievable outdoors |
| Doubling time, outdoor practice | 3 – 5 days | |

**⚠ Be sceptical of published yields.** Figures of 25–30 g/m²/day exist but come
from short runs, in ideal seasons, at exceptional sites. Annual averages are what
pay for a facility. Our own design basis uses **12 g/m²/day peak and 7 g/m²/day
annual mean**, and even those are assumptions awaiting a cultivation trial.

### 4.3 Working density

| Point | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Harvest trigger | 0.65 g/L |
| Acceptable harvest range | 0.50 – 0.90 g/L |
| Density after harvest | ≈ 0.55 g/L |
| **Minimum working density** | **0.30 g/L** |
| Above ~1.0 g/L | Light-limited; growth per unit area falls |

**⚠ Never harvest below 0.30 g/L.** A thin culture has lost its self-shading, is
exposed to full sun, and is wide open to contamination because there is nothing
competing for the light. Recovering a pond from over-harvest takes longer than
the extra biomass was worth.

## 5. The culture medium

### 5.1 Zarrouk's medium — the reference

Zarrouk's medium is the standard reference formulation for *Arthrospira*. It is

| Component | g/L |
| --- | --- |
| Sodium bicarbonate, NaHCO₃ | 16.8 |
| Di-potassium phosphate, K₂HPO₄ | 0.5 |
| Sodium nitrate, NaNO₃ | 2.5 |
| Potassium sulphate, K₂SO₄ | 1.0 |
| Sodium chloride, NaCl | 1.0 |
| Magnesium sulphate, MgSO₄·7H₂O | 0.2 |
| Calcium chloride, CaCl₂·2H₂O | 0.04 |
| Ferrous sulphate, FeSO₄·7H₂O | 0.01 |
| EDTA (disodium) | 0.08 |
| A5 micronutrient solution | 1 mL/L |

**A5 micronutrient solution** (per litre of stock):

| Component | g/L of stock |
| --- | --- |
| Boric acid, H₃BO₃ | 2.86 |
| Manganese chloride, MnCl₂·4H₂O | 1.81 |
| Zinc sulphate, ZnSO₄·7H₂O | 0.222 |
| Copper sulphate, CuSO₄·5H₂O | 0.079 |
| Sodium molybdate, Na₂MoO₄·2H₂O | 0.039 |

### 5.2 Why we do not use full Zarrouk commercially

**Cost, almost entirely bicarbonate.** At 16.8 g/L, a single 100 m³ pond needs
1.68 tonnes of sodium bicarbonate to fill. That is not economic, and most of it
is not consumed — it is a buffer reservoir, not a feedstock.

The commercial approach:

| Change | Reason |
| --- | --- |
| Bicarbonate reduced to 10 – 12 g/L | Still an effective buffer and barrier; large cost saving |
| Carbon supplied as **CO₂**, bicarbonate as make-up only | CO₂ regenerates the bicarbonate consumed; far cheaper per kg of carbon |
| Nitrogen **dosed to demand**, not held at 2.5 g/L | Nitrate is dosed against uptake, not maintained at laboratory concentration |
| Other salts roughly halved | Adequate at working concentrations |
| Composition corrected for **source water** | Hard water already supplies calcium and magnesium; dosing the standard recipe on top over-supplies them |

**⚠ Never dose a standard recipe into water of unknown composition.** If the
borewell already carries 200 mg/L of calcium, adding the Zarrouk calcium
chloride on top will precipitate carbonate, consume alkalinity, scale equipment
and cloud the culture. Get the water analysed and subtract what it already
provides.

### 5.3 Making up medium — order matters

**⚠ Add salts in the right order or you will precipitate the medium.**

1. Start with the required volume of **treated water**.
2. Begin circulation before adding anything.
3. Dissolve **bicarbonate first**, fully.
4. Add **nitrate**, then **sulphates**, then **chloride**.
5. Add **phosphate slowly and last among the majors**, with the water moving.
6. Add **iron pre-chelated with EDTA** — dissolve the EDTA first, then the iron
   into it, then add the combined solution.
7. Add **micronutrients** last.
8. Check pH; adjust with bicarbonate or carbonate.
9. Confirm alkalinity by titration before inoculating.

| Mistake | Result |
| --- | --- |
| Phosphate added to concentrated calcium | Calcium phosphate precipitates — a white cloud, phosphorus gone |
| Iron added without chelation | Iron hydroxide precipitates at pH 10 — a rusty floc, iron unavailable |
| Salts added to still water | Local supersaturation and precipitation at the point of addition |
| Bicarbonate added last | Other salts precipitate before the buffer is established |

### 5.4 Reagent quality

**⚠ Every reagent that enters the pond ends up in the food.**

| Requirement | Reason |
| --- | --- |
| Food-grade reagents | Heavy metals in a reagent concentrate into the biomass |
| Certificate of analysis per lot | Including lead, cadmium, mercury and arsenic |
| No fertiliser-grade substitution | Agricultural-grade salts routinely carry heavy metals at levels irrelevant to plants and unacceptable in food |
| Segregated storage | Away from cleaning chemicals |
| Dry storage | Several reagents are hygroscopic and cake in monsoon humidity |

## 6. Starting and scaling a culture

### 6.1 The scale-up train

Never inoculate a production pond directly from a laboratory flask. Step it up,
roughly tenfold at a time, verifying at every step.

| Step | Volume | Environment | Typical residence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Master culture | 250 mL – 1 L | Laboratory, controlled, **kept in duplicate in two locations** | Maintained indefinitely |
| Working culture | 5 – 20 L | Laboratory | 7 – 14 days |
| Starter tank 1 | 200 L | Closed tank | 7 – 14 days |
| Starter tank 2 | 2 m³ | Closed tank | 7 – 14 days |
| Nursery pond | 10 m³ / 50 m² | Covered pond | 10 – 20 days |
| Production pond | 100 m³ / 435 m² | Open raceway | Semi-continuous |

**⚠ Keep the master culture in duplicate, in two physically separate places.** It
is the genetic starting material for the entire facility. A single freezer
failure, fire or contamination event should not be able to destroy it.

### 6.2 Release criteria — do not skip these

A culture moves to the next stage **only** when it passes every one:

| Check | Requirement |
| --- | --- |
| Microscopy | Characteristic *A. platensis* helical morphology dominant |
| Contaminating phototrophs | Below 5 % of field |
| **Grazers** | **None detected — this is absolute** |
| Density | At or above the transfer density for that step |
| Filament condition | Intact, well-coiled, no widespread fragmentation or lysis |
| Colour | Strong blue-green |
| pH behaviour | Rising in daylight, confirming active growth |
| Records | Transfer authorised, recorded, batch number assigned |

**⚠ A single rotifer transferred into a production pond can collapse it inside
three days.** Checking takes ten minutes. Restarting a pond takes three weeks.

### 6.3 Inoculating a production pond

1. Clean and disinfect the pond; rinse thoroughly.
2. Fill with prepared medium (§ 5.3) to about **half** operating depth.
3. Confirm pH, alkalinity and temperature are in range.
4. Start circulation.
5. Add inoculum to give a starting density of at least **0.1 – 0.15 g/L**.
6. Run at reduced depth until density reaches ~0.3 g/L, then fill to full depth.
7. Do not harvest until density is stable at 0.5 g/L or above.
8. Microscopy daily from day one.

**Starting shallow is deliberate**: less volume to fill, higher starting density
for the same inoculum, faster establishment, and less dilute culture sitting in
full sun.

# How do I run the ponds each day?

The daily routine, from the morning walk to the evening check. How to take
each measurement properly — Secchi depth, dry weight, pH, and the daily
microscopy that is the most valuable ten minutes in the whole operation.
How to feed a pond, and what has to be written down.

## 7. The daily routine

| Time | Task |
| --- | --- |
| **Early morning** | Walk every pond. Check circulation running, paddle wheels sound, no scum, no unusual colour or smell. Record pH and temperature. |
| **Mid-morning** | Take samples from every pond. Secchi depth. Microscopy. Decide today's harvests. |
| **Late morning** | Harvest. Density is at its daily peak and temperature has not yet peaked. |
| **Midday** | Process the harvest: wash, dewater, press, load the dryer. Watch pond temperature on hot days. |
| **Afternoon** | Nutrient dosing into moving water. Complete records. Clean equipment. |
| **Evening** | Final walk. Confirm circulation. Confirm CO₂ dosing has stopped for the night. |
| **Weekly** | Alkalinity titration, gravimetric dry weight, probe calibration, velocity check, deep-clean equipment. |

### 7.1 The morning walk — what to look at

| Look for | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Culture colour | Deep blue-green good. Yellow-green = nitrogen or iron short. Pale = too dilute or bleached. Brown = dying |
| Surface scum or foam | Circulation problem, over-mature culture, or contamination |
| Clarity at the edge | Sudden clearing means a crash is starting |
| Smell | Healthy culture smells faintly of the sea. Sour, sulphurous or septic means anaerobic material |
| Wall and corner deposits | Settled biomass; a contamination reservoir. Clean it |
| Paddle wheel sound | Bearing noise, rubbing, uneven splash |
| Flow at the bends | Look for a dead zone or a rotating eddy |
| Insects on the surface | Larvae source; act now |
| Bird activity | Contamination and pest vector |

## 8. Measurements — how to actually take them

### 8.1 Secchi depth — the daily density check

The routine field method. A weighted white disc is lowered until it disappears.

| Step | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Same person, same time of day, same spot, shade the surface with your body |
| 2 | Lower the disc slowly until the pattern just vanishes |
| 3 | Record the depth in centimetres |
| 4 | Convert using **your own** calibration curve |

| Secchi depth | Approximate density | Action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| > 15 cm | < 0.3 g/L | ⚠ Too thin — do not harvest |
| 10 – 15 cm | 0.3 – 0.5 g/L | Growing; leave it |
| 5 – 8 cm | 0.5 – 0.8 g/L | Harvest range |
| < 4 cm | > 0.9 g/L | Over-dense; harvest now |

**⚠ Those figures are indicative only.** Secchi depth must be correlated against
gravimetric dry weight for *your* culture, and re-correlated after any change of
strain, medium or depth. A borrowed correlation is a guess.

### 8.2 Gravimetric dry weight — the reference method

1. Filter a measured volume (typically 100–500 mL) through a pre-dried, pre-weighed filter.
2. **Wash the retained biomass with distilled water** — ⚠ skip this and you weigh the medium salts too, which at 18 g/L TDS is a very large error.
3. Dry at 105 °C to constant weight (typically 2–4 h).
4. Cool in a desiccator, weigh.
5. Density (g/L) = (final mass − filter mass) in g ÷ sample volume in L.

Run weekly minimum, and always when re-establishing the Secchi correlation.

### 8.3 pH

| Point | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| Method | In-line probe, continuous, per pond |
| Calibration | **Weekly minimum**, two-point, buffers 7 and 10 |
| Why so often | ⚠ High alkalinity and constant biofouling drift these probes fast |
| Cleaning | Inspect and clean the bulb at every calibration |
| Cross-check | Bench meter on a grab sample |

A pH probe reading 9.5 when the culture is at 10.3 will cause the control system
to withhold CO₂ and starve the pond of carbon. Calibration is not paperwork.

### 8.4 Alkalinity

Titrate weekly with standard acid to the phenolphthalein and methyl orange
endpoints. Report as g/L NaHCO₃ equivalent. Falling alkalinity means bicarbonate
make-up is needed.

### 8.5 Salinity

Conductivity meter, daily. Rising steadily is normal — evaporation concentrates
salts. Approaching 30 g/L means blowdown is due (§ 12.4).

### 8.6 Microscopy — the most valuable ten minutes of the day

**⚠ Daily microscopy on every production pond. This is not optional and it is
not a laboratory nicety — it is the control that saves ponds.**

A rotifer population is detectable under a microscope **two to four days before**
it is visible as a falling density. By the time you can see the problem with the
naked eye, you have usually lost the pond.

| Step | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| Sample | Fresh, from the moving stream, not the edge |
| Magnification | 100× to scan, 400× to identify |
| Scan | At least ten fields, systematically |
| Record | Every day, even when normal — you need the baseline to see a trend |

What to record:

| Observation | Normal | Action limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Spirulina morphology | Well-coiled helices, mixed lengths | Widespread uncoiling or fragmentation |
| Other phototrophs | Occasional cells | **> 5 % of field** |
| **Rotifers** | **None** | **Any — act immediately** |
| Amoebae, ciliates | None | Any |
| Insect larvae | None | Any |
| Cell lysis, empty sheaths | Rare | Frequent |
| Bacterial clouds | Light | Heavy, especially around debris |

## 9. Feeding the ponds

**Principle: replace what you removed.**

Every kilogram of dry biomass harvested takes its elemental composition out of
the pond (§ 3.5). Dose that back, then verify by analysis.

| Rule | Reason |
| --- | --- |
| Dose **in solution**, never as solids into the pond | Undissolved solids sink, dissolve locally, create extreme concentrations |
| Dose into **moving water** downstream of the paddle wheel | Distribution |
| ⚠ **Never dose when circulation is stopped** | Creates a zone of lethal concentration and extreme pH — this is interlocked in the control system for a reason |
| Dose **little and often** | Especially critical for urea |
| **Verify by laboratory analysis**, not by dose record | A dosing pump that has failed still writes a perfect record |
| Prepare in a dedicated tank | Not in a bucket, not in a harvest vessel |

Nutrient dosing is deliberately **not** automated at this facility. The governing
measurement is a laboratory analysis, not a live signal, and an automatic system
dosing against a wrong assumption compounds the error for days before anyone
notices.

## 10. Records

If it is not written down, it did not happen. Minimum daily record per pond:

- date, time, operator
- pH morning and afternoon
- temperature morning and afternoon
- Secchi depth and derived density
- microscopy result, including "normal"
- volume harvested
- nutrients dosed: reagent, lot number, quantity
- CO₂ consumed
- make-up water added
- observations: colour, smell, foam, weather, anything unusual

These records are what make a batch traceable, and they are the first thing an
auditor or a serious customer will ask to see.

# How do I harvest and process it?

From pond to packed powder. When to harvest and how much to take, why the pump
must be gentle, how screening works and why it is so much cheaper than
centrifuging, washing and pressing, and the drying step where product quality
is most easily destroyed.

## 11. Harvesting

### 11.1 When

| Factor | Guidance |
| --- | --- |
| Density | At or above 0.65 g/L |
| Time of day | Late morning |
| Why | Density peaks after a morning of photosynthesis, before peak temperature |
| Weather | Do not harvest immediately before heavy rain — dilution on top of a low density is compounding two problems |
| How much | Take density down to about 0.55 g/L, never below 0.30 g/L |

### 11.2 The steps

```
Pond  →  pump  →  screen  →  wash  →  dewater  →  press  →  wet cake  →  dryer
          ↓         ↓
      low shear   filtrate returns to pond
```

### 11.3 Pumping — handle it gently

**⚠ The pump handles living biomass. Filament damage is lost yield and lost
pigment.**

| Requirement | Reason |
| --- | --- |
| Low-shear pump type | Centrifugal pumps at high speed shred filaments |
| Modest flow velocity | Shear rises steeply with velocity |
| Smooth pipework, gentle bends | Every sharp change in direction is a shear point |
| No throttling on the suction | Cavitation destroys filaments |

Fragmented filaments pass straight through the harvest screen. They are gone —
into the filtrate and back to the pond, where they lyse and feed bacteria.

### 11.4 Screening

| Parameter | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Screen aperture | 30 – 50 µm |
| Hydraulic loading | 5 – 15 m³/h per m² of screen |
| Output | ≈ 30 g/L slurry (about 3 % solids) |
| Capture efficiency | ≥ 92 % |

**⚠ Inspect the screen at every harvest.** A torn screen passes biomass to the
filtrate, and the loss is invisible until you reconcile yields at month end.
Screens must be cleanable without tools; a screen that is awkward to clean will
not be cleaned.

<figure class="diagram" data-diagram="screening">
<figcaption>Live: why screening works. Long helical filaments cannot pass a 30–50 µm mesh and are retained; water and anything single-celled goes straight through. This is the whole reason spirulina needs no centrifuge.</figcaption>
</figure>

### 11.5 Washing

Harvested biomass carries culture medium with it — mostly sodium bicarbonate,
sodium nitrate and sodium chloride. Unwashed, that medium dries into the product.

| Consequence of poor washing | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| High ash | ⚠ Fails the ≤ 9 % specification |
| High sodium | Nutritionally undesirable, and detectable |
| Salty taste | Immediately noticed by customers |

| Parameter | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Wash water | Treated, potable standard |
| Wash ratio | ≈ 2 L per kg of wet cake |
| Verification | Product ash on the certificate of analysis |

Over-washing wastes treated water, creates more saline effluent, and begins to
leach soluble intracellular material. Optimise against the ash specification.

### 11.6 Dewatering and pressing

| Stage | In | Out |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Screening | 0.65 g/L | ≈ 3 % solids |
| Dewatering | 3 % | 10 – 15 % |
| Pressing | 10 – 15 % | **18 – 22 %** |

**⚠ Do not over-press.** Excess pressure ruptures filaments and releases
phycocyanin into the press liquor.

**The operator check: look at the press liquor.** It should be pale straw or
faintly green. **If it is blue or blue-green, you are pressing product into the
drain.** Reduce pressure or duration.

### 11.7 The wet cake is perishable

Wet cake at 20 % solids, warm, alkaline and nutrient-rich, is an ideal bacterial
substrate.

| Condition | Maximum hold |
| --- | --- |
| Ambient | **2 hours** |
| Chilled at ≤ 5 °C | 8 hours |
| Preferred | **Straight to the dryer, no storage** |

**⚠ The gap between press and dryer is the highest microbiological risk in the
entire process.** Size the dryer so a peak harvest never queues.

## 12. Drying — where quality is won or lost

### 12.1 The rule

| Parameter | Limit |
| --- | --- |
| **Maximum product temperature** | **60 °C** |
| Brief excursion | 65 °C |
| Target final moisture | 5 % |
| Maximum final moisture | 7 % |

Phycocyanin is heat labile. Every degree and every minute above the limit costs
colour, and colour is a large part of the value.

### 12.2 The trap at the end of the cycle

While free water is evaporating, **evaporative cooling holds the product well
below the air temperature**. Air at 90 °C can dry product sitting at 50 °C.

When the free water is gone, that cooling disappears **and the product
temperature runs up to air temperature within minutes.**

**⚠ This is where batches are ruined.** Everything looks fine for hours, then the
last part of the cycle destroys the pigment.

<figure class="diagram" data-diagram="dryingtrap">
<figcaption>Live: the temperature trap. While free water evaporates it cools the product, holding it far below the air temperature. When the water runs out that cooling vanishes and product temperature climbs through the 60 °C limit within minutes.</figcaption>
</figure>

| Requirement | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| Measure **product** temperature, not air temperature | Air temperature tells you nothing about the product once cooling stops |
| Control on product temperature | The end of the cycle is the design case |
| Record every cycle | Time, temperature, batch |
| Stop at target moisture | Over-drying is wasted energy and lost pigment |

### 12.3 Methods

| Method | Assessment |
| --- | --- |
| **Spray drying** | Industry standard at scale. Contact time in seconds. Excellent colour retention. High capital cost; needs a more dilute feed than our press produces |
| **Low-temperature air drying of extruded biomass** | Well suited to this scale. Biomass extruded into noodles to raise surface area, dried at 50–60 °C |
| **Solar-assisted with controlled auxiliary heat** | Lowest energy cost. Must be enclosed for hygiene. Monsoon performance must be proven |
| **Open sun drying** | ⚠ **NOT ACCEPTABLE.** No temperature control, no contamination control, no records, no traceability. This is how traditional dihé is made; it is not how food-grade product is made |

### 12.4 Hygiene during drying

- Enclosed, pest-proofed space
- Filtered drying air
- Food-grade contact surfaces
- Time, temperature and batch recorded for every cycle

## 13. Milling, sieving and packing

### 13.1 Milling

| Parameter | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Mill type | Impact or pin mill, food grade |
| Target particle size | 95 % passing 180 µm (80 mesh) |
| ⚠ Product temperature in milling | Must not exceed 60 °C |
| Metal detection | Downstream of milling |
| Dust extraction | Required — hygiene and safety |

Milling generates heat. A mill running hot undoes the care taken in drying.

### 13.2 Humidity

**⚠ Spirulina powder is strongly hygroscopic.** Dried product left in open
containers in monsoon humidity will reabsorb moisture past specification within
hours, and must then be re-dried — a second thermal exposure and more lost
pigment.

Milling, sieving, blending and packing all take place in humidity-controlled
space, and dried product is never left open.

### 13.3 Blending

Blend to homogenise a batch **before** sampling. A sample from an unblended batch
does not represent it, and the certificate of analysis is then meaningless.

### 13.4 Packing

| Requirement | Reason |
| --- | --- |
| Moisture barrier | Hygroscopic |
| Oxygen barrier | Phycocyanin oxidises |
| Light barrier | Phycocyanin is photodegraded |
| Nitrogen flush | Optional; extends shelf life |
| Batch code on every pack | Traceability |

### 13.5 Storage

| Condition | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| Cool, dark, dry, sealed | Product holds 24 months |
| Warm | Pigment degrades |
| Light | Pigment degrades |
| Humid | Caking, then microbial growth |
| Open container | All of the above |

# How do I know it is safe to sell?

The product specification and the testing behind it. Microcystins — the one
genuine safety hazard associated with commercial spirulina, and why every
single batch is tested for them. And the batch records that make a recall
bounded rather than catastrophic.

## 14. Product specification

| Parameter | Specification | Method |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Appearance | Fine dark blue-green powder | Visual |
| Moisture | ≤ 7 % | Gravimetric |
| Crude protein | ≥ 55 % (typical 60–65 %) | Kjeldahl, N × 6.25, factor stated |
| Phycocyanin | ≥ 8 % (premium grade) | Spectrophotometric |
| Total ash | ≤ 9 % | Gravimetric |
| Acid-insoluble ash | ≤ 1 % | Gravimetric |
| Particle size | 95 % passing 180 µm | Sieve analysis |

### 14.1 Microbiological

| Parameter | Limit |
| --- | --- |
| Total plate count | ≤ 100,000 cfu/g |
| Yeast and mould | ≤ 500 cfu/g |
| Coliforms | ≤ 10 cfu/g |
| *E. coli* | Absent in 1 g |
| *Salmonella* | Absent in 25 g |
| *Staphylococcus aureus* | Absent in 1 g |

### 14.2 Contaminants

| Parameter | Limit |
| --- | --- |
| Lead | ≤ 3.0 mg/kg |
| Cadmium | ≤ 1.0 mg/kg |
| Mercury | ≤ 0.1 mg/kg |
| Arsenic | ≤ 2.0 mg/kg |
| **Microcystins** | **≤ 1.0 µg/g** |

## 15. Microcystins — the one that really matters

**⚠ Read this section properly. It is the difference between a food business and
a liability.**

| Fact | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| *Arthrospira platensis* **does not produce microcystins** | The organism you are growing is not the hazard |
| Other cyanobacteria **do** | *Microcystis*, *Anabaena*, *Planktothrix* and others produce potent hepatotoxins |
| Open ponds can be invaded by them | Wind, birds, dust, water, equipment |
| Microcystins are heat stable | Drying does not destroy them |
| They are the principal recognised safety hazard of commercially sold spirulina | Contaminated product has repeatedly been found on international markets |

**Therefore:**

1. **Microcystin is a release parameter for every batch.** No exceptions.
2. The contamination controls of § 3.4 and § 17 exist largely to prevent the
   organisms that produce it from ever establishing.
3. Daily microscopy (§ 8.6) is your early warning.
4. If a toxin-producing cyanobacterium is identified in a pond, that pond's
   biomass does not enter the food chain. Not reprocessed, not blended away,
   not sold as feed. Dumped, and the pond cleaned and restarted.

This is the reason the alkaline, high-pH regime is treated as sacred throughout
this manual. It is not just yield protection. It is toxin prevention.

## 16. Batch traceability

Every finished pack must be traceable **backwards** to the pond it came from, and
every pond traceable **forwards** to every pack containing its biomass.

Forward traceability is what makes a recall bounded. Without it, one suspect
pond implicates every pack you have ever shipped.

| Record | Contents |
| --- | --- |
| Source pond | Identity, harvest dates |
| Inoculum | Batch and transfer records |
| Culture conditions | pH, temperature, density over the growth period |
| Nutrients | Reagent, lot, quantity, date |
| Harvest | Date, volume, operator |
| Washing and dewatering | Date, water quality, operator |
| Drying | Cycle, time, temperature trace |
| Milling and blending | Date, equipment, operator |
| Testing | All § 14 results |
| Release | Authorisation and date |
| Packing | Date, format, quantity, pack codes |

**No batch is packed for sale until every § 14 parameter is measured, in
specification, and release is authorised and recorded.** The certificate of
analysis states the **measured values for that batch**, not the specification
limits.

# What do I do when something goes wrong?

Contamination, grazers, and the rotifer emergency that can take a pond in three
days. A symptom-to-cause diagnostic table covering colour, smell, foam, density
and lost pigment. And how to decide when a pond is no longer worth saving.

## 17. Contamination

### 17.1 What can invade

| Type | Examples | Threat |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Competing phototrophs | *Chlorella*, *Scenedesmus*, *Oscillatoria*, *Phormidium* | Product purity; possible toxin producers |
| **Grazers** | **Rotifers (*Brachionus*)**, amoebae, ciliates | ⚠ Fastest and most destructive — can collapse a pond in 48–72 h |
| Insect larvae | Chironomid (bloodworm), mosquito | Grazing and physical contamination |
| Bacteria and fungi | Various | Product microbiology |
| Foreign matter | Dust, debris, birds | Contamination |

### 17.2 Rotifers — the emergency

Rotifers eat spirulina filaments. They reproduce explosively. **A pond can go
from healthy to unrecoverable in three days.**

**Signs, in order of appearance:**

1. Rotifers under the microscope — visible days before anything else
2. Filament fragments and empty sheaths increasing
3. Density stops rising despite good conditions
4. pH stops climbing in daylight
5. Culture visibly clearing
6. Collapse

**Response — act on step 1, not step 5:**

| Option | Method | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Harvest hard** | Take the culture down and dilute the population | First response, always available, no risk |
| **Raise pH** | Push to 10.8–11.0 briefly with carbonate | Rotifers are less tolerant than spirulina. ⚠ Stressful for the culture too |
| **Raise alkalinity and salinity** | Towards the top of the operating range | Slower, gentler |
| **Ammonia shock** | Brief controlled free-ammonia dose | ⚠ **High risk.** Can kill the culture. Last resort, calculated, never improvised |
| **Dump and restart** | Empty, clean, disinfect, re-inoculate | Sometimes the cheapest option — take it early rather than after two weeks of failed rescue |

**⚠ Never move water, nets, sampling gear or hands from an affected pond to a
healthy one.** Dedicated equipment per pond, or cleaned and disinfected between.

### 17.3 Competing algae

| Sign | Response |
| --- | --- |
| Green (not blue-green) tint | Raise pH and alkalinity to the top of range |
| *Chlorella* under microscope | Increase harvest rate — spirulina screens out, *Chlorella* passes through, so harvesting selectively removes your crop... but it also removes nutrients that feed the competitor |
| Persistent above 5 % of field | Consider dumping the pond |

The chemistry is the main weapon. If competitors are establishing, the first
question is always: **has alkalinity or pH drifted low?**

### 17.4 Insect larvae

Find and remove the breeding site — standing water anywhere on site. Screen and
cover the nursery ponds. Check the pond edges and any wet corners.

## 18. Culture problems — diagnostic table

| Symptom | Likely causes | Check | Action |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Yellow-green culture** | Nitrogen deficiency; iron deficiency | Nitrate and iron analysis | Dose nitrogen; check iron is chelated |
| **Pale, washed out** | Over-harvested; photoinhibition | Density | Stop harvesting; increase depth |
| **Growth stopped, pH flat in daylight** | Carbon exhausted; culture failing | pH trend, alkalinity, CO₂ system | Check CO₂ supply and probe calibration |
| **pH climbing past 11** | Carbon demand exceeds CO₂ supply | CO₂ flow | Increase CO₂; check injection efficiency |
| **pH falling** | CO₂ over-dose; culture dying | Microscopy | Reduce CO₂; investigate the culture |
| **Density falling** | Grazers; over-harvest; crash | ⚠ **Microscopy first, always** | Per § 17.2 |
| **Filaments fragmenting** | Pump shear; stress; grazers | Microscopy; check pump and velocity | Reduce shear; check conditions |
| **Uncoiled straight filaments** | Temperature, nutrient or chemical stress | Full parameter check | Correct the stressor |
| **Surface foam** | Circulation fault; lysing cells; over-mature | Circulation; microscopy | Check paddle wheels; harvest |
| **Settled deposits at bends** | Dead zone in the channel | Flow at the bends | ⚠ Geometry fault — clean now, fix the civil design |
| **Sour or septic smell** | Anaerobic deposits | Corners, sumps, dead zones | Clean thoroughly |
| **Rusty precipitate** | Iron added unchelated | Dosing procedure | Pre-chelate iron with EDTA |
| **White cloudiness** | Carbonate or phosphate precipitation | Water hardness; dosing order | Review § 5.3 |
| **Sudden clearing after rain** | Dilution | Alkalinity, salinity, density | Restore chemistry; do not harvest |
| **Product dull or olive** | Drying too hot; storage exposure | Drying records | ⚠ Review § 12 |
| **Product tastes salty** | Under-washing | Ash result | Increase wash ratio |
| **Powder caking** | Moisture pickup | Moisture; humidity control | Review § 13.2 |

## 19. Deciding to dump a pond

Hard to do, but often correct. Dump when:

- A toxin-producing cyanobacterium is identified — **not negotiable**
- Grazers have established and two interventions have failed
- Density is below 0.2 g/L and falling
- Contamination is above 20 % of field
- The cause cannot be identified and the culture continues to decline

**⚠ Rejected culture is never transferred to another pond to save it.** That is
how one lost pond becomes four.

Disposal is via the process effluent route — high pH, high biomass, and it may
not be discharged untreated.

Then: clean, disinfect, rinse, refill, re-inoculate from the verified train
(§ 6), and **write down what you think caused it**. The record is how you avoid
a repeat.

## 22. Worked calculations

### 22.1 Daily production from areal productivity

```
Production (kg/day) = pond area (m²) × productivity (g/m²/day) ÷ 1000
```

Stage 1 peak: 1,750 m² × 12 g/m²/day ÷ 1000 = **21 kg/day**

### 22.2 Harvest volume

```
Volume (m³) = biomass (kg) ÷ [ density (kg/m³) × capture efficiency ]
```

21 kg ÷ (0.65 × 0.92) = **35.1 m³/day**

Sanity check: 35.1 ÷ 350 m³ pond volume = **10 % of pond volume per day.**
Commercial practice is 8–20 %. ✓

### 22.3 Nutrient dosing from harvest

For 21 kg/day of dry biomass:

| Element | Demand | Reagent | Dose |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| N | 21 × 0.105 = 2.21 kg | Sodium nitrate | 2.21 ÷ 0.165 = **13.4 kg/day** |
| N (alternative) | 2.21 kg | Urea | 2.21 ÷ 0.466 = **4.7 kg/day** |
| P | 21 × 0.011 = 0.23 kg | K₂HPO₄ | 0.23 ÷ 0.178 = **1.30 kg/day** |
| Mg | 21 × 0.003 = 0.063 kg | MgSO₄·7H₂O | 0.063 ÷ 0.099 = **0.64 kg/day** |
| Fe | 21 × 0.001 = 0.021 kg | FeSO₄·7H₂O | 0.021 ÷ 0.201 = **0.10 kg/day** |

⚠ The phosphate above also supplies 1.30 × 0.448 = 0.58 kg K against a demand of
0.32 kg. **Reduce potassium sulphate accordingly or potassium accumulates.**

### 22.4 CO₂ demand

```
CO₂ = biomass × carbon fraction × (44 ÷ 12) ÷ utilisation efficiency
```

21 × 0.46 × 3.667 ÷ 0.65 = **54.5 kg/day**

The 44/12 is the molar mass ratio of CO₂ to carbon. The utilisation efficiency is
why contactor depth matters: surface sparging achieves only 30–40 %, which nearly
doubles the gas bill.

### 22.5 Wet cake and drying load

| Step | Calculation | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Wet cake at 20 % solids | 21 ÷ 0.20 | 105 kg/day |
| Product at 5 % moisture | 21 ÷ 0.95 | 22.1 kg/day |
| Water to evaporate | 105 − 22.1 | **82.9 kg/day** |
| Over an 8 h cycle | 82.9 ÷ 8 | 10.4 kg water/h |
| Thermal duty at 5,000 kJ/kg | 10.4 × 5000 ÷ 3600 | **14.4 kW** |

### 22.6 Evaporation make-up

```
Make-up (m³/day) = pond area (m²) × evaporation (mm/day) ÷ 1000
```

Peak: 1,750 × 10 ÷ 1000 = **17.5 m³/day**

This is about 80 % of total process water demand. Evaporation, not process use,
sizes the water supply.

## 24. Glossary

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Alkalinity** | Capacity of the medium to neutralise acid; here from carbonate and bicarbonate |
| **Areal productivity** | Biomass produced per square metre of pond surface per day |
| ***Arthrospira*** | The correct genus of commercial spirulina |
| **Baffle wall** | The central dividing wall of a raceway pond |
| **Blowdown** | Deliberate removal of culture medium to control salinity build-up |
| **Carbonation sump** | A deepened section of the channel where CO₂ is dissolved |
| **Filtrate** | Liquid passing through the harvest screen; returned to the pond |
| **Gas vesicles** | Gas-filled protein structures giving buoyancy |
| **Hormogonium** | A short motile filament fragment that grows into a new trichome |
| **Linear morphotype** | Uncoiled, straight form of the trichome; a stress indicator |
| **Necridium** | A specialised dying cell that lets a trichome fragment |
| **Photoinhibition** | Damage and reduced photosynthesis caused by excess light |
| **Phycocyanin** | The blue, water-soluble, heat-labile pigment |
| **Raceway** | An oval circulating pond driven by a paddle wheel |
| **Secchi depth** | Depth at which a white disc disappears; a field density proxy |
| **Trichome** | A multicellular filament |
| **Zarrouk's medium** | The standard reference growth medium |

## 25. Ten rules

1. **Circulation never stops.**
2. **Microscopy every day, every pond, recorded even when normal.**
3. **Never harvest below 0.30 g/L.**
4. **Never let pH drop below 8.8 — it is the contamination barrier.**
5. **Never dose nutrients into a still pond.**
6. **Never let product temperature exceed 60 °C in drying.**
7. **Never transfer anything from a sick pond to a healthy one.**
8. **Never release a batch without a microcystin result.**
9. **Never claim spirulina provides vitamin B12, and never make a health claim.**
10. **Write it down. If it is not recorded, it did not happen.**
